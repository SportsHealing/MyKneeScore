Know your knee

Four bones, four ligaments, two kinds of cartilage.

The knee is a hinge that also rotates, which is why it is easy to injure. This page covers each structure, the job it does, and the problems it tends to develop. Use it to make sense of a diagnosis or a scan report.

Structure one

Bones

Four bones meet at or near the knee. The femur and tibia form the main joint, the patella glides across the front, and the fibula sits to the outside as an anchor for soft tissue.

Bone

Femur

The thigh bone. Its lower end widens into two rounded knuckles, the condyles, which roll and glide on the top of the tibia.

What can go wrong

  • Fracture. Usually a high force injury in younger people, or a low force fall in thinner, osteoporotic bone.
  • Osteochondritis dissecans. A small segment of bone and its overlying cartilage loses blood supply and can loosen. Seen most in teenagers.
  • Osteonecrosis. Sudden pain on the inner side, often at night, as a patch of bone loses its blood supply.
  • Bone bruising. Deep swelling within the bone after impact. Painful for weeks, but it settles.

Bone

Tibia

The shin bone. Its flat top, the tibial plateau, takes most of your body weight when you stand and walk.

What can go wrong

  • Plateau fracture. The top surface is crushed or split. Alignment matters here, so these often need fixing.
  • Stress fracture. A hairline crack from repeated loading. Common when running mileage rises too fast.
  • Osgood-Schlatter disease. Pain and a bump just below the kneecap in growing teenagers, where the patellar tendon pulls on the bone.
  • Bone marrow lesions. Areas of swollen bone under worn cartilage. A frequent source of pain in arthritis.

Bone

Patella

The kneecap. It sits in a groove on the femur and works as a lever, making the thigh muscle far more efficient at straightening the knee.

What can go wrong

  • Dislocation. The kneecap slips sideways, almost always to the outside. It often relocates itself as the knee straightens.
  • Maltracking. The kneecap runs off centre in its groove, causing pain at the front of the knee on stairs and after sitting.
  • Fracture. Usually from a direct fall onto the front of the knee.
  • Chondromalacia. Softening of the cartilage on the back of the kneecap, giving grinding and aching.

Bone

Fibula

The slim outer bone of the lower leg. It carries little weight. Its main role at the knee is anchoring the lateral ligament and a hamstring tendon.

What can go wrong

  • Head fracture. Often occurs alongside a serious ligament injury on the outer side of the knee.
  • Nerve injury. The common peroneal nerve wraps around the fibula. Damage here can cause numbness and difficulty lifting the foot.
  • Stress fracture. Uncommon, and linked to repetitive impact sport.

Structure two

Ligaments

Ligaments are strong bands joining bone to bone. Four control the knee. Two cross inside the joint, and two sit on either side. Injuries are graded one to three: stretched, partly torn, or fully torn.

Ligament

Anterior cruciate (ACL)

Runs diagonally through the centre of the joint. It stops the tibia sliding forwards and controls twisting.

What can go wrong

  • Rupture. Typically a pivot, a sudden stop, or an awkward landing. Many people hear or feel a pop.
  • Rapid swelling. The knee fills with blood within a few hours, which is a strong clue to the diagnosis.
  • Giving way. Weeks later the knee feels unstable on turning, even once swelling has gone.
  • Associated damage. Meniscal tears and cartilage bruising are common at the same time.

Ligament

Posterior cruciate (PCL)

The thicker of the two central ligaments. It stops the tibia sliding backwards under the femur.

What can go wrong

  • Direct impact injury. A blow to the front of the bent knee, classically against a dashboard or from falling onto the knee.
  • Vague symptoms. Aching and stiffness rather than dramatic instability, so it is easy to miss.
  • Late kneecap pain. Long standing PCL laxity increases load through the front of the knee.
  • Recovery. Isolated tears often do well with rehabilitation rather than surgery.

Ligament

Medial collateral (MCL)

A broad band on the inner side. It resists forces that push the knee inwards into a knock-kneed position.

What can go wrong

  • Sprain or tear. Caused by a blow to the outside of the knee, or a ski edge catching.
  • Tenderness along the inner line. Pain sits over the ligament rather than deep in the joint.
  • Good healing. The MCL has a decent blood supply and usually recovers with a brace and physiotherapy.
  • Stiffness. Scarring can limit full bending if early movement is avoided.

Ligament

Lateral collateral (LCL)

A cord-like band on the outer side, running from the femur to the head of the fibula. It resists forces pushing the knee outwards.

What can go wrong

  • Tear. Less common than MCL injury, and usually from a blow to the inner side of the knee.
  • Combined injury. Often part of wider damage to the posterolateral corner, which needs specialist assessment.
  • Nerve symptoms. Numbness or foot drop can follow, because the peroneal nerve runs close by.
  • Instability. Untreated tears leave the knee feeling like it opens up on uneven ground.

Structure three

Cartilage

The knee has two different types. Articular cartilage coats the bone ends. The menisci are separate wedges that sit between them. Neither has much blood supply, so both heal slowly.

Cartilage

Articular cartilage

A smooth layer a few millimetres thick covering the ends of the femur, the top of the tibia and the back of the patella. It lets the joint slide with almost no friction.

What can go wrong

  • Osteoarthritis. Gradual thinning and loss of the surface, leading to stiffness, aching and reduced range of movement.
  • Focal defect. A single pothole in the surface, often after injury. It can cause catching and swelling in an otherwise healthy knee.
  • Poor healing. There is no blood supply and no nerve supply, so damage does not repair itself and early loss is silent.
  • Crepitus. Grating or crunching as roughened surfaces pass over one another.

Cartilage

Meniscus

Two C-shaped wedges of tough fibrocartilage, one on each side. They spread load across the joint, absorb shock and add stability.

What can go wrong

  • Acute tear. A twist on a planted foot, common in sport. Pain on the joint line, swelling over a day or so, and clicking.
  • Degenerate tear. Wear related splitting from the age of about forty. Often no clear injury.
  • Locking. A displaced flap blocks the knee so it cannot fully straighten. This needs prompt assessment.
  • Repair prospects. Only the outer third has a blood supply, so tears there can be stitched. Inner tears usually cannot.
  • Root tear. The meniscus detaches at its attachment and slips out of the joint, which speeds up arthritis.

Do not wait

When to seek help the same day

Most knee pain can be managed in your own time. These signs cannot.

  • The knee is hot, red and swollen, with or without a fever.
  • You cannot put weight through the leg after an injury.
  • The knee is locked and will not fully straighten.
  • The leg looks bent or out of line after a fall or collision.
  • The knee swelled up within an hour of an injury.
  • You have numbness, pins and needles, or a cold foot.
